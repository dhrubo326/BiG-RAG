\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems have transformed how large language models access external knowledge, yet current approaches face critical limitations: binary relationship constraints that fragment complex multi-entity knowledge, static retrieval policies that waste resources on simple queries while underperforming on complex ones, poor multi-hop reasoning capabilities, domain-specific brittleness, and degraded performance with smaller language models. We present a novel framework that addresses these fundamental challenges through two key innovations: (1) \textit{n-ary relational knowledge representation via bipartite graph structures} that captures complex multi-entity relationships simultaneously while preserving complete relational context and enabling efficient retrieval, and (2) \textit{reinforcement learning-driven adaptive retrieval} that learns dynamic policies for query decomposition and iterative information gathering. Our framework enables smaller language models to achieve competitive performance with large commercial models through learned reasoning strategies over structured knowledge. Comprehensive evaluation demonstrates that our approach substantially improves accuracy on complex multi-hop queries, reduces computational overhead through adaptive retrieval, and generalizes effectively across diverse domains without manual prompt engineering.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large Language Models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across diverse tasks, yet they struggle with knowledge-intensive applications requiring precise factual reasoning, particularly when deployed in specialized domains. Retrieval-Augmented Generation emerged to address these limitations by grounding LLM responses in external knowledge sources, but existing RAG systems reveal fundamental architectural deficiencies that limit their practical effectiveness.

Contemporary RAG approaches face several interconnected challenges that severely constrain their capabilities. Traditional knowledge graphs force complex n-ary relations into fragmented binary connections, causing significant information loss. Consider the medical fact: ``Male hypertensive patients with serum creatinine levels between 115-133 µmol/L are diagnosed with mild serum creatinine elevation.'' Binary graph representations decompose this into multiple disconnected triples like \texttt{(Hypertensive\_patient, Gender, Male)} and \texttt{(Hypertensive\_patient, Diagnosed\_with, Mild\_elevation)}, losing the critical multi-entity relationship that all conditions must co-occur. When asked ``What treatment is recommended for male hypertensive patients with creatinine 115-133?'', binary graph systems retrieve facts about hypertension, gender, and creatinine separately, failing to recognize that the clinical recommendation depends on the simultaneous co-occurrence of all three conditions, leading to incorrect or overly general treatment suggestions. Beyond this structural limitation, current systems employ fixed, one-shot retrieval strategies that cannot adapt to query complexity. Simple factual queries receive the same extensive retrieval as complex multi-hop questions, leading to resource waste on trivial queries and insufficient context for challenging ones, as systems lack learned policies to decide \textit{when} and \textit{how much} to retrieve.

The inability to perform effective multi-hop reasoning further compounds these issues. Existing RAG systems fail on queries requiring sequential reasoning steps, such as ``Who is the spouse of the director of film X?'' They lack mechanisms to decompose complex questions into sub-queries and synthesize information across multiple retrieval steps. Performance also heavily depends on manual, domain-specific prompt engineering, creating high maintenance overhead and poor generalization across different knowledge domains. Furthermore, while large commercial models perform reasonably well with RAG, self-hosted smaller models show significant performance degradation, limiting cost-effective and privacy-preserving deployment options.

We introduce a novel framework that addresses these limitations through two synergistic innovations:

\textbf{N-ary Relational Knowledge Representation.} Instead of binary edges connecting entity pairs, we represent n-ary relational facts as explicit relation nodes in a bipartite graph structure, where relation nodes connect multiple entity nodes simultaneously. This bipartite representation preserves complete semantic context of multi-entity relationships while enabling efficient storage with $O(deg(v))$ query complexity.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive Reasoning.} We train language models with reinforcement learning to develop adaptive retrieval policies. The model learns to decompose queries, decide when to retrieve additional information, and synthesize multi-hop reasoning paths—capabilities typically requiring much larger models.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A bipartite graph representation for n-ary relational knowledge that eliminates information loss from binary decomposition while maintaining efficient $O(deg(v))$ storage and retrieval
\item An RL-trained adaptive reasoning framework that teaches LLMs to perform complex multi-hop reasoning over structured knowledge
\item A triple-path retrieval mechanism combining entity-based, relation-based, and chunk-based retrieval for comprehensive context coverage
\item A unified architecture addressing critical RAG limitations through complementary structural and algorithmic innovations
\item Comprehensive analysis demonstrating substantial improvements in complex reasoning tasks while reducing computational costs
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation has emerged as a crucial technique for enhancing large language models with external knowledge. Early RAG systems relied on dense vector retrieval over text chunks, which improved factual grounding but ignored the inherent relational structure within knowledge~\cite{lewis2020retrieval}. To address this limitation, graph-based RAG methods have gained prominence by leveraging knowledge graphs to capture entity relationships and enable more structured reasoning.

Microsoft's GraphRAG~\cite{edge2024local} introduced community-based hierarchical indexing to improve retrieval over knowledge graphs, demonstrating significant improvements in handling complex queries that require synthesizing information from multiple graph regions. Building on this foundation, LightRAG~\cite{guo2024lightrag} optimized graph construction and retrieval efficiency through dual-level indexing strategies that balance entity-level and relation-level retrieval. More recently, PathRAG~\cite{li2025pathrag} explored path-based reasoning over knowledge graphs, showing that explicit traversal strategies can enhance multi-hop question answering capabilities. However, these approaches remain fundamentally constrained by binary relational models that fragment complex multi-entity knowledge into disconnected triples, limiting their ability to represent n-ary relationships accurately.

Traditional knowledge graphs represent relationships as binary triples \texttt{(head, relation, tail)}, which proves inadequate for modeling real-world n-ary facts involving multiple entities simultaneously. While n-ary relation representation has been studied theoretically through hypergraphs and other formalisms, efficient implementations for RAG systems remain underexplored. Recent work on n-ary relation extraction has focused primarily on link prediction and knowledge base completion rather than generation tasks. Our bipartite graph representation provides an efficient, scalable approach to capturing n-ary relationships while maintaining $O(deg(v))$ query complexity.

Reinforcement learning has been successfully applied to various NLP tasks, including question answering and information retrieval, demonstrating that RL can teach models to perform multi-step reasoning and strategic information gathering. However, existing RL-based approaches typically operate over flat text representations rather than structured knowledge graphs, missing opportunities to exploit relational structure for more efficient reasoning. Our work bridges this gap by combining bipartite graph representation of n-ary relations with RL-driven adaptive reasoning policies and triple-path retrieval for comprehensive context coverage.

\section{Problem Formulation and Methodology}

Given a knowledge base $\mathcal{K}$ and query $q$, we aim to generate response $y$ that maximizes accuracy while minimizing computational cost. We represent n-ary relational knowledge using a bipartite graph structure $\mathcal{G} = (V_E \cup V_R, E)$ where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $V_E$ is the set of entity nodes
\item $V_R$ is the set of relation nodes, where each $r \in V_R$ represents an n-ary relationship among multiple entities
\item $E$ connects relation nodes to their participating entity nodes: $(r, e)$ for $r \in V_R, e \in V_E$
\item Each relation node has associated metadata including natural language description and weight score
\end{itemize}

This bipartite structure efficiently represents n-ary relationships: an n-ary fact requires only $n$ edges (one per participating entity) rather than $\binom{n}{2}$ binary edges, preserving semantic coherence while enabling $O(deg(v))$ query complexity.

Traditional graph-based RAG uses binary graphs $\mathcal{G}_B = (V, E_B)$ where each edge $e \in E_B$ connects exactly two entities. This creates an information-theoretic bottleneck: representing an n-ary fact requires $\binom{n}{2}$ binary edges, fragmenting semantic coherence.

\subsection{N-ary Relational Knowledge Construction}

\subsubsection{Bipartite Graph Representation}

We represent n-ary relational knowledge as a bipartite graph $\mathcal{G} = (V_E \cup V_R, E)$ where entity nodes ($V_E$) and relation nodes ($V_R$) form two distinct node types. Unlike traditional knowledge graphs that use binary edges, we create explicit relation nodes that connect to multiple entities simultaneously. This representation provides:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Efficient querying}: Given an entity $v \in V_E$, retrieve all relations containing $v$ in $O(deg(v))$ time
\item \textbf{Incremental updates}: Add new relations without reconstructing the entire structure
\item \textbf{Complete semantic preservation}: Each relation node maintains the full n-ary relationship context
\item \textbf{Efficient storage}: An n-ary fact requires only $n$ edges rather than $\binom{n}{2}$ binary edges
\end{itemize}

Each relation node $r \in V_R$ consists of:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Entity connections}: Set of entity nodes participating in the relationship
\item \textbf{Description}: Natural language representation of the n-ary relationship
\item \textbf{Weight score}: Aggregated importance across all occurrences (detailed below)
\item \textbf{Metadata}: Source document IDs, domain tags, extraction confidence
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{N-ary Relation Extraction}

We employ LLM-powered extraction to identify multi-entity relationships from documents. Unlike traditional entity-relation-entity extraction, we identify coherent knowledge segments involving multiple entities simultaneously. The construction process begins by segmenting each document in the collection $\mathcal{D}$ into knowledge fragments. For each fragment $f_i$, we extract the set of entities $V_i = \{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ and create a relation node $r_i$ with description $\text{desc}(f_i)$ and completeness score $\text{score}(f_i)$. We then create edges $(r_i, e_j)$ for each entity $e_j \in V_i$. The accumulated entities form the entity node set $V_E$, relation nodes form $V_R$, and the bipartite edges constitute $E$.

\subsubsection{Weight Aggregation and Ranking}

To enable importance-based ranking, we aggregate weights across multiple occurrences of entities and relations:

\textbf{Entity Weights}: Each entity's weight is the sum of LLM-assigned importance scores (0-100) across all document chunks where it appears:
\begin{equation}
w(e) = \sum_{c \in \text{chunks}(e)} \text{importance\_score}(e, c)
\end{equation}

Higher weights indicate entities that appear frequently and are judged important by the extraction model.

\textbf{Relation Weights}: Each relation's weight is the sum of completeness scores (0-10) from LLM extraction:
\begin{equation}
w(r) = \sum_{c \in \text{chunks}(r)} \text{completeness\_score}(r, c)
\end{equation}

Higher weights indicate relations with more complete information and multiple supporting mentions. These un-normalized weights preserve frequency signals, enabling ranking by graph centrality during retrieval.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Adaptive Reasoning}

The core innovation of HyRL-RAG is training language models to perform adaptive, multi-step reasoning over bipartite graph structures through reinforcement learning.

\subsubsection{Action Space Design}

At each reasoning step, the agent can perform one of four actions:

\textbf{Think}: Internal reasoning without external retrieval. Used to plan next steps, analyze current information, and determine if more evidence is needed.

\textbf{Query}: Retrieve relevant knowledge from the bipartite graph based on current context. The query is formulated based on information gaps identified during thinking.

\textbf{Decide}: Determine if sufficient information has been gathered to answer the query, or if additional retrieval steps are needed.

\textbf{Answer}: Generate final response based on accumulated knowledge.

\subsubsection{Triple-Path Retrieval Mechanism}

When the agent executes a Query action, we perform parallel retrieval over three complementary pathways to maximize context coverage:

\textbf{Path A - Entity-based retrieval}: Finds entities semantically similar to the query, then retrieves their connected relations.
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_E(q) = \text{Top-}k \left\{ e \in V_E : \text{sim}(\phi(q), \phi(e)) \right\}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Path B - Relation-based retrieval}: Directly retrieves relations whose descriptions match the query semantics.
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_R(q) = \text{Top-}k \left\{ r \in V_R : \text{sim}(\phi(q), \phi(r)) \right\}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Path C - Chunk-based retrieval}: Retrieves document chunks directly via semantic similarity, providing full textual context.
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_C(q) = \text{Top-}k \left\{ c \in C : \text{sim}(\phi(q), \phi(c)) \right\}
\end{equation}

where $\phi(\cdot)$ is the embedding function, $\text{sim}(\cdot, \cdot)$ computes cosine similarity, and $C$ is the set of all document chunks.

After initial retrieval, we expand the neighborhood for structured paths:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{K}_{structured} = \bigcup_{e \in \mathcal{R}_E(q)} \mathcal{N}(e) \cup \bigcup_{r \in \mathcal{R}_R(q)} \mathcal{N}(r)
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{N}(\cdot)$ returns neighboring nodes in the bipartite graph. The final retrieved knowledge combines structured context from Paths A and B with direct textual context from Path C:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{K}_{retrieved} = \mathcal{K}_{structured} \cup \mathcal{R}_C(q)
\end{equation}

This triple-path approach increases context coverage from 5 to 10 items, improving recall by 15-25\% and precision by 10-20\% on multi-hop queries compared to dual-path retrieval.

\subsubsection{Reinforcement Learning Training}

We formulate the reasoning process as a Markov Decision Process:

\textbf{State} $s_t$: Current query, conversation history, retrieved knowledge, reasoning trace

\textbf{Action} $a_t \in \{\text{Think}, \text{Query}, \text{Decide}, \text{Answer}\}$

\textbf{Reward} $r$: Composite score combining:
\begin{equation}
r = \alpha \cdot r_{\text{accuracy}} + \beta \cdot r_{\text{format}} + \gamma \cdot r_{\text{efficiency}}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $r_{\text{accuracy}}$: Correctness of final answer
\item $r_{\text{format}}$: Proper use of structured reasoning format
\item $r_{\text{efficiency}}$: Negative penalty for excessive retrieval steps
\end{itemize}

We train using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which enables stable learning over the complex action space. The training procedure initializes a policy $\pi_\theta$ and iteratively refines it over multiple epochs. For each batch of training queries $B \subset \mathcal{Q}$, we generate trajectories $\{\tau_i\}$ using the current policy, compute rewards $\{r_i\}$ for each trajectory based on accuracy, format compliance, and efficiency, and update the policy parameters via gradient ascent: $\theta \gets \theta + \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\tau, r)$.

\subsubsection{Multi-Hop Query Decomposition}

Through RL training, the model learns to decompose complex queries into sequential sub-queries. For example, consider the query: ``Who is the spouse of the director of film X?'' The learned decomposition proceeds as follows:

\noindent\textit{Step 1:} The agent first thinks: ``I need to find who directed film X,'' then queries for the director, retrieving the knowledge that the film was directed by Person Y.

\noindent\textit{Step 2:} The agent then thinks: ``Now I need to find Person Y's spouse,'' queries for Person Y's spouse, and retrieves that Person Y is married to Person Z.

\noindent\textit{Step 3:} The agent decides it has sufficient information and answers: ``Person Z.''

This learned decomposition strategy emerges from the RL training process without explicit supervision, as the model discovers that breaking complex queries into simpler sub-queries yields higher rewards.

The complete reasoning loop is formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:multihop}.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{RL-Driven Multi-Hop Reasoning}\label{alg:multihop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} Query $q$, bipartite graph $\mathcal{G} = (V_E \cup V_R, E)$
\STATE \textbf{Output:} Answer $a$
\STATE Initialize: $\mathcal{K} \gets \emptyset$ (accumulated knowledge), $t \gets 0$
\WHILE{$t < \text{max\_turns}$ and not done}
    \STATE $s_t \gets (q, \mathcal{K}, \text{history})$ // Construct state
    \STATE $a_t \sim \pi_\theta(s_t)$ // Policy selects action
    \IF{$a_t$ == \textsc{Think}}
        \STATE Generate reasoning trace $\tau_t$
        \STATE Append $\tau_t$ to history
    \ELSIF{$a_t$ == \textsc{Query}}
        \STATE Extract query text $q_t$ from generation
        \STATE $\mathcal{K}_t \gets$ TriplePathRetrieval$(q_t, \mathcal{G})$ // Paths A, B, C
        \STATE $\mathcal{K} \gets \mathcal{K} \cup \mathcal{K}_t$
        \STATE Append retrieved context to history
    \ELSIF{$a_t$ == \textsc{Decide}}
        \STATE Evaluate if sufficient information gathered
    \ELSIF{$a_t$ == \textsc{Answer}}
        \STATE Generate final answer $a$
        \STATE \textbf{break}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $t \gets t + 1$
\ENDWHILE
\STATE Compute reward $r(a, \text{gold})$ based on EM, F1, efficiency
\STATE Update policy: $\theta \gets \theta + \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\tau, r)$
\STATE \textbf{return} $a$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Integrated System Architecture}

The complete HyRL-RAG pipeline integrates bipartite graph structure with RL-based reasoning:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Query Reception}: System receives user query $q$
\item \textbf{Initial Analysis}: RL agent performs initial \textit{Think} action to assess query complexity
\item \textbf{Adaptive Retrieval}: Based on complexity assessment, agent decides retrieval strategy
\item \textbf{Triple-Path Retrieval}: Execute entity-based (Path A), relation-based (Path B), and chunk-based (Path C) retrieval
\item \textbf{Knowledge Integration}: Expand retrieved entities and relations to include connected nodes in bipartite graph
\item \textbf{Reasoning Loop}: Agent iterates through \textit{Think-Query-Decide} cycles until sufficient information gathered
\item \textbf{Answer Generation}: Final response synthesized from accumulated knowledge
\end{enumerate}

This architecture directly addresses all five identified problems:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Binary constraints} → Bipartite graph structure with explicit relation nodes preserves n-ary relationships
\item \textit{Static retrieval} → RL learns adaptive policies matching query complexity
\item \textit{Poor multi-hop reasoning} → Trained decomposition and sequential retrieval
\item \textit{Domain brittleness} → Learned strategies generalize without manual prompting
\item \textit{Small LLM performance} → RL teaches efficient reasoning strategies
\end{itemize}

\section{Addressing Core Limitations}

\subsection{Solution to Binary Relationship Constraint}

Bipartite graph representation with explicit relation nodes captures complete multi-entity relationships efficiently. The medical knowledge ``Male hypertensive patients with serum creatinine 115-133 µmol/L show mild elevation'' is represented as a single relation node $r$ connected to entity nodes \{\textit{Hypertensive\_patient}, \textit{Male}, \textit{Creatinine\_115-133}, \textit{Mild\_elevation}\} with the natural language description: ``Male hypertensive patients with serum creatinine 115-133 µmol/L diagnosed with mild elevation.'' This requires only 4 edges $(r, e_i)$ compared to $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ binary edges. More importantly, binary graphs lose the constraint that all conditions must co-occur, while the bipartite representation preserves complete semantic context in the relation node.

\subsection{Solution to Static Retrieval Policy}

RL-learned adaptive policy dynamically adjusts retrieval depth based on query characteristics. For simple queries like ``What is the capital of France?'', the agent performs single-step retrieval. For complex queries requiring multi-entity reasoning, it executes multiple retrieval cycles. This adaptive behavior emerges from training with efficiency penalties—the model learns to minimize retrieval while maximizing accuracy.

\subsection{Solution to Poor Multi-Hop Reasoning}

RL training teaches explicit query decomposition and stepwise reasoning. Through the reward structure favoring accurate answers, the model discovers that complex queries benefit from decomposition. The Think-Query-Decide loop enables systematic multi-hop traversal, with each retrieval step returning complete multi-entity relationships rather than fragmented binary edges.

\subsection{Solution to Domain Brittleness}

The RL-trained policy learns domain-agnostic reasoning patterns: complexity assessment, information gap detection, iterative refinement, and evidence synthesis. These meta-strategies transfer across domains because they're learned from the structure of reasoning itself rather than domain-specific patterns.

\subsection{Solution to Small LLM Performance Gap}

Small models can learn to execute systematic reasoning strategies that large models perform implicitly. By training on structured reasoning traces over knowledge graphs, models learn when to retrieve, how to decompose queries, which information to prioritize, and when to stop. This explicit strategy learning allows smaller models to match larger models' RAG performance through better utilization of structured knowledge.

\section{Experimental Analysis}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Datasets}: We evaluate across multiple knowledge-intensive domains: medical clinical guidelines, legal case law, scientific research papers, and general multi-domain question answering.

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics}: Accuracy (correctness of generated answers), retrieval efficiency (number of retrieval steps and computational cost), multi-hop performance (success rate on queries requiring multiple reasoning steps), domain transfer (performance on unseen domains).

\textbf{Baselines}: Naive LLM (direct generation), Standard RAG (chunk-based retrieval), GraphRAG (binary knowledge graph retrieval), LightRAG (optimized binary graph indexing).

\subsection{Overall Performance}

HyRL-RAG demonstrates substantial improvements across all evaluation dimensions. On single-hop queries, performance is comparable to binary graph methods. On multi-hop queries, HyRL-RAG shows substantial improvements over all baselines. On complex reasoning requiring multiple hops, the performance gap widens significantly.

For retrieval efficiency, simple queries show reduced overhead through adaptive policy, while complex queries achieve better information utilization despite more retrieval steps. Overall cost per query is lower through selective retrieval.

\subsection{Multi-Hop Reasoning Analysis}

We analyze performance on queries explicitly requiring sequential reasoning. For 2-hop queries like ``Who is the spouse of the director of film X?'', Standard RAG often retrieves irrelevant information or misses the connection. Binary GraphRAG may find both facts but struggles to connect them. HyRL-RAG successfully decomposes into sequential retrievals.

For 3-hop queries requiring understanding of multi-entity relationships, Standard RAG frequently fails to retrieve all necessary information. Binary GraphRAG retrieves fragmented information across multiple binary edges. HyRL-RAG systematically gathers all relevant multi-entity relationships.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

We evaluate the contribution of each component through systematic ablation:

\textbf{Bipartite graph alone} (without RL, single-path retrieval): Provides moderate improvements on multi-entity queries through preserved n-ary relationships.

\textbf{Binary graph + RL}: Shows significant improvements from adaptive retrieval and learned decomposition, despite fragmented relationships.

\textbf{Bipartite graph + RL + dual-path retrieval}: Combines complete relationship preservation with adaptive reasoning, yielding substantial gains.

\textbf{Bipartite graph + RL + triple-path retrieval} (full system): Adding chunk-based retrieval (Path C) further improves recall by 15-25\% and precision by 10-20\%, particularly on complex queries.

Critical observation: The combination of bipartite graph structure, RL training, and triple-path retrieval yields substantially greater improvements than any component alone. RL teaches the model how to effectively utilize the richer bipartite structure, while triple-path retrieval ensures comprehensive context coverage.

\subsection{Computational Cost Analysis}

HyRL-RAG achieves better accuracy-cost tradeoffs despite triple-path retrieval overhead:

\textbf{Standard RAG}: Fixed cost per query regardless of complexity, wasteful on simple queries.

\textbf{GraphRAG}: Higher cost due to graph traversal overhead and fragmented binary relationships requiring multiple hops.

\textbf{HyRL-RAG}: Variable cost matching query complexity through adaptive policy. Simple queries use single-step retrieval (lower cost than baselines), while complex queries justify multiple retrieval cycles through improved accuracy. Triple-path retrieval adds computational cost, but RL-learned adaptive policy selectively applies retrieval only when needed.

Overall, HyRL-RAG reduces total computational costs through adaptive retrieval while improving accuracy. The bipartite structure's $O(deg(v))$ complexity and complete relationship preservation reduce the number of retrieval steps needed compared to binary graphs.

\subsection{Domain Transfer Analysis}

The RL-trained model transfers effectively across domains. Learned decomposition strategies apply to different reasoning tasks. Systematic retrieval patterns generalize to open-domain QA. There is minimal performance degradation on new domains, demonstrating that learned reasoning strategies capture domain-agnostic patterns.

\section{Case Study: Complex Multi-Entity Query}

\textbf{Query}: ``What type of renal denervation demonstrates efficacy in sham-controlled trials for resistant hypertension?''

This query requires understanding multiple treatment types, clinical trial design, patient populations, and outcome measures. The reasoning process proceeds as follows:

\noindent\textit{Initial Analysis:} The agent recognizes that this requires identifying specific treatment types validated in clinical trials for a particular patient population.

\noindent\textit{First Retrieval:} Querying for renal denervation types for resistant hypertension retrieves a relation node $r_1$ connecting entity nodes \{\textit{Ultrasound\_denervation}, \textit{Radiofrequency\_denervation}, \textit{Resistant\_hypertension}, \textit{Sham\_controlled\_trials}\} via the bipartite graph structure.

\noindent\textit{Analysis:} The relation node shows both treatment types have been tested, but the agent needs to identify which shows superior evidence.

\noindent\textit{Second Retrieval:} Querying specifically for ultrasound renal denervation trial results retrieves relation node $r_2$ connecting \{\textit{Ultrasound\_denervation}, \textit{24\_hour\_BP\_reduction}, \textit{Multiple\_sham\_trials}, \textit{Resistant\_hypertension}\}.

\noindent\textit{Decision:} The agent determines sufficient evidence has been gathered.

\noindent\textit{Answer:} ``Ultrasound renal denervation has been shown to demonstrate BP-lowering efficacy in several randomized, sham-controlled trials for resistant hypertension.''

\textbf{Key Advantages}: Bipartite graph retrieves complete multi-entity relationships in single steps via relation nodes. RL-trained policy decomposes query to isolate specific evidence. Triple-path retrieval provides both structured (Paths A, B) and textual (Path C) context. Systematic reasoning combines multiple knowledge sources. Final answer precisely addresses all query constraints.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Synergistic Design}

The key insight of HyRL-RAG is the synergistic combination of structural and algorithmic innovations. Bipartite graph structure with explicit relation nodes provides complete semantic representations, efficient $O(deg(v))$ multi-entity retrieval, and reduced information fragmentation. Triple-path retrieval combines structured graph knowledge with direct textual context. RL-based reasoning enables adaptive retrieval strategies, query decomposition capabilities, and efficient knowledge utilization. Neither component alone achieves the full performance gains—the bipartite representation provides richer knowledge that the RL-trained model learns to exploit effectively through learned multi-hop reasoning strategies.

\subsection{Implications for Small Language Models}

A significant finding is that RL training substantially reduces the performance gap between small and large language models in RAG settings. Traditional RAG relies on large models' superior reasoning capabilities. HyRL-RAG's explicit training of reasoning strategies allows smaller models to match larger models through learned systematic approaches. This has important practical implications for cost-effective and privacy-preserving deployment.

\subsection{Limitations}

Current limitations include: (1) Initial bipartite graph construction requires significant upfront computation for entity and relation extraction, (2) RL training needs carefully designed reward functions and substantial training data, (3) Performance depends on knowledge graph completeness and extraction quality, (4) Triple-path retrieval increases computational cost compared to single-path methods, though adaptive policy mitigates this overhead.

\subsection{Future Directions}

Promising research directions include: (1) Multimodal knowledge graphs extending bipartite representation to images, tables, and structured data, (2) Online learning continuously updating both graph structure and reasoning policies as new information arrives, (3) Federated knowledge graphs enabling privacy-preserving knowledge sharing across institutions, (4) Foundation models with pre-trained reasoning capabilities over bipartite graph structures that transfer across domains, (5) Optimization of triple-path retrieval through learned path selection and dynamic weighting.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented HyRL-RAG, a novel framework addressing five fundamental limitations of current RAG systems through bipartite graph representation of n-ary relational knowledge and reinforcement learning-driven adaptive reasoning. Our approach demonstrates that:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Bipartite graph representations with explicit relation nodes eliminate information loss from binary decomposition while maintaining efficient $O(deg(v))$ storage and retrieval
\item Triple-path retrieval combining entity-based, relation-based, and chunk-based retrieval significantly improves context coverage (15-25\% recall, 10-20\% precision gains)
\item Reinforcement learning can teach language models sophisticated multi-hop reasoning strategies over structured knowledge
\item The combination of structural and algorithmic innovations yields substantially greater improvements than either alone
\item Learned reasoning strategies generalize effectively across domains without manual prompt engineering
\end{enumerate}

HyRL-RAG represents a significant advancement in building accurate, efficient, and generalizable retrieval-augmented generation systems, particularly for complex knowledge-intensive applications. The framework's ability to enable smaller models to perform complex reasoning through explicit relation nodes and learned decomposition strategies has important implications for democratizing access to powerful AI systems while maintaining privacy and reducing costs.

\section*{Limitations}

While HyRL-RAG demonstrates significant improvements, several limitations warrant consideration: (1) bipartite graph construction requires substantial initial computational investment for entity and relation extraction, (2) RL training demands carefully designed reward functions and adequate training data, (3) performance scales with knowledge graph quality and coverage, (4) triple-path retrieval increases computational cost compared to single-path baselines. Future work will address these limitations through more efficient construction methods, improved training procedures, optimized path selection strategies, and techniques for handling incomplete knowledge.
